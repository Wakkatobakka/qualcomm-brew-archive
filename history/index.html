Platform history

BREW reached players through phone carriers. Their storefronts, device lists and testing programs shaped which games a handset owner could download.

The store on the handset

On a Verizon phone in 2002, a game download started in a menu called the BREW Shop. Verizon took the service nationwide in June and renamed it Get It Now by September. Get Games sat alongside menus for pictures, ringtones and other applications. The carrier ran the download server and handled the bill.[1]

Qualcomm had announced the Binary Runtime Environment for Wireless the year before as an application environment for CDMA devices. Its developer kit, handset software and distribution system served different parts of the same process. A studio could write a game against Qualcomm’s tools, while a wireless carrier decided how to sell it on the phones it supported.[2][1]

KDDI’s shop in Japan

KDDI and Okinawa Cellular announced their first BREW phone, Toshiba’s A5304T, in January 2003. The commercial service opened on February 21. It gave Japanese publishers a route to au customers on KDDI’s network. A Japanese EZweb listing and a Western review can describe different mobile adaptations of the same series, as the Katamari record shows.[3][4]

Testing before a download could go on sale

Summus submitted a text-and-image application to TRUE BREW testing in December 2001. The testing lab, NSTL, sent back a list of items to address. In January the company told shareholders it had made revisions and planned to resubmit, while it evaluated a game for certification. In the same letter the developers were pursuing a separate J2ME port.[5]

Making 3D run on the phone

When Qualcomm promoted OpenGL ES support on its mobile chipsets in March 2005, Gameloft named Asphalt: Urban GT and Ghost Recon among the games it was developing with the technology. The archive also has OpenGL ES examples and QX samples, whose filenames and contents give a different view of the platform from the retail games.[6][7]

A Qualcomm conference talk took developers through a 3D test scene called Neocore. The QX toolset included Maya and 3ds Max exporters, a particle editor and a texture optimizer. The talk reports the scene going from 5–10 to 30 frames per second on the test chipset after tuning. Texture compression, mesh layout and the decision to skip objects outside the view were among the steps shown.[8]
